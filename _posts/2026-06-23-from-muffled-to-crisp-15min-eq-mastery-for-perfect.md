### Step-by-Step Tutorial: Master Your EQ in 15 Minutes

#### Step 1: Understand the Frequency Spectrum
What to do: Learn the five key frequency ranges.
What you'll see: The 10-band EQ in Volume Booster - Sound Booster spans 31 Hz to 16 kHz.
- **Sub-bass (20-60 Hz)**: Felt more than heard (e.g., kick drum thump).
- **Bass (60-250 Hz)**: Warmth and body (e.g., bass guitar).
- **Mids (250 Hz-2 kHz)**: Vocals and instruments (e.g., guitar, piano).
- **Upper mids (2-4 kHz)**: Clarity and presence (e.g., vocals cutting through).
- **Treble (4-16 kHz)**: Air and sparkle (e.g., cymbals, hi-hats).

Common error: Boosting everything equally creates a "loudness war" that distorts. Focus on cutting problem frequencies first.

#### Step 2: Open Volume Booster - Sound Booster and Load Your Track
What to do: Launch the app, then play your test track in any music player (e.g., Spotify).
What you'll see: The app's main screen shows a 10-band EQ graph with sliders. A "Volume Boost" slider sits below. Tap the EQ icon to activate.

I remember my first time: I saw 10 sliders and froze. But the app's presets—like "Rock" or "Jazz"—make it easy. Don't skip this step: ensure your track is playing so you hear changes in real time.

#### Step 3: Apply a Neutral Preset to Establish a Baseline
What to do: Tap the "Presets" dropdown and select "Flat" (or "Normal").
What you'll see: All sliders snap to 0 dB. This removes any prior adjustments.

Why it matters: A flat EQ reveals your headphones' true sound. If they sound tinny, you'll know to cut treble later. This baseline prevents overcorrection.

#### Step 4: Cut Problem Frequencies First (The Secret to Clear Audio)
What to do: Lower sliders at frequencies that sound harsh or boomy.
What you'll see: Drag the 250 Hz slider down by 3 dB if vocals sound muddy. Reduce 4 kHz by 2 dB if cymbals hurt your ears.

For example, on my cheap earbuds, I cut 125 Hz by 4 dB to reduce boominess. The app's real-time feedback is instant. Common error: Cutting too much—start with 2 dB and adjust.
