## Step-by-Step Tutorial: The Best Equalizer Settings for Different Music Genres

### Step 1: Open Volume Booster - Sound Booster and Access the Equalizer
Launch the app. You’ll see a clean interface with a big volume dial, bass and treble sliders, and a button labeled **Equalizer**. Tap it. If you don’t see the equalizer, check for an update in the Play Store. Common error: some devices hide the EQ behind a “More” menu—look for three dots. Once open, you’ll see a 5-band or 10-band equalizer with sliders for frequencies like 60Hz, 230Hz, 910Hz, 3.6kHz, and 14kHz. Don’t panic—we’ll adjust them step by step.

### Step 2: Start with a Flat Baseline
Before customizing, reset all sliders to 0 dB (the middle position). This is your neutral starting point. Why? Because you need to hear the original sound before enhancing it. Tap the **Reset** button if available. I remember the first time I skipped this step—my settings were a mess from previous tweaks, and I couldn’t tell what was working. Now, I always start flat. You’ll hear the raw audio, which might sound dull—that’s normal.

### Step 3: Set Up a Pop Music Preset
Pop music relies on vocals and punchy beats. Use these settings:
- 60Hz: +2 dB (adds warmth)
- 230Hz: -1 dB (reduces muddiness)
- 910Hz: +1 dB (clarifies vocals)
- 3.6kHz: +3 dB (presence for vocals)
- 14kHz: +2 dB (air and sparkle)

In Volume Booster - Sound Booster, drag the sliders to these values. Then, play a pop track like “Blinding Lights” by The Weeknd. You’ll notice the vocals cut through and the kick drum feels tighter. Common mistake: boosting 60Hz too much makes the bass boomy. Stick to +2 dB.

### Step 4: Rock and Metal Settings for Guitar Crunch
Rock needs midrange bite and controlled bass. Adjust to:
- 60Hz: +1 dB
- 230Hz: -2 dB (reduces boxiness)
- 910Hz: +4 dB (guitar presence)
- 3.6kHz: +2 dB (attack)
- 14kHz: +1 dB (cymbals)

Play “Bohemian Rhapsody” by Queen. The guitars should sound aggressive without piercing your ears. If it’s too harsh, lower 910Hz to +2 dB. Volume Booster - Sound Booster’s real-time preview lets you hear changes instantly—no need to restart the song.

### Step 5: Hip-Hop and EDM: Deep Bass Without Distortion
Bass is king here, but clarity matters too. Try:
- 60Hz: +5 dB (sub-bass rumble)
- 230Hz: -3 dB (clean up mud)
- 910Hz: +1 dB
- 3.6kHz: +2 dB (snare snap)
- 14kHz: +3 dB (hi-hats)

For EDM, push 60Hz to +6 dB and 14kHz to +4 dB. Test with “Sicko Mode” by Travis Scott or “Strobe” by deadmau5. Warning: excessive bass boost can cause distortion on phone speakers. Use headphones. If you hear crackling, reduce 60Hz by 1–2 dB. Volume Booster - Sound Booster’s built-in limiter helps prevent clipping, but moderation is key.

### Step 6: Classical and Jazz: Natural and Balanced
Classical music demands a flat response with slight warmth. Use:
- 60Hz: 0 dB
- 230Hz: +1 dB (body)
- 910Hz: 0 dB
- 3.6kHz: +1 dB (string detail)
- 14kHz: +2 dB (air)

For jazz, boost 230Hz to +2 dB and 3.6kHz to +2 dB. Play “Clair de Lune” or “Take Five.” The instruments should sound like they’re in the room with you. Avoid boosting bass—it ruins the natural timbre. I once over-boosted and the cello sounded like a fuzzy mess. Lesson learned.

### Step 8: Fine-Tune for Your Headphones
Every headphone has a different frequency response. If your headphones are bass-heavy, reduce 60Hz by 1–2 dB. If they’re bright, lower 14kHz. Volume Booster - Sound Booster’s 10-band EQ gives you precision. Take 5 minutes to calibrate for your specific gear. This step alone can make a $30 pair of earbuds sound like $100 ones.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid
1. **Boosting all frequencies** – This just makes everything louder and muddier. Cut before you boost.
2. **Using phone speakers for EQ** – Phone speakers can’t reproduce deep bass; you’ll overcompensate and ruin headphone sound. Always use headphones.
3. **Ignoring the source quality** – Streaming at 128 kbps sounds bad no matter the EQ. Use at least 256 kbps or lossless.
4. **Over-boosting bass** – +10 dB at 60Hz causes distortion and hearing damage. Keep it under +6 dB.
